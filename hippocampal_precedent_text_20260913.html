1. Kember is the closest precedent for the continuous similarity idea

Kember et al. calculate the neocortical connectivity profile of every hippocampal vertex and correlate it with each of the three system templates. They then use the maximum correlation for the categorical assignment. ⓝ Nature

So mathematically they already have:

\[ r_A(v),\; r_B(v),\; r_P(v) \]

before reducing them to:

\[ \arg\max[r_A, r_B, r_P]. \]

That's important because the continuous information we're discussing isn't something foreign to their algorithm. It's already generated by template matching; they simply use it primarily to determine system membership.

What I have not found in Kember is the next step we're discussing:

\[ \Delta r(v) = r_{\mathrm{top1}}(v) - r_{\mathrm{top2}}(v) \]

and then testing whether that functional differentiation/assignment margin differs between clinical and control populations.

That looks like a genuinely interesting extension.

2. Lynch et al. is probably your strongest precedent for the HC–MDD topography comparison

Lynch et al. did something remarkably analogous in cortex/striatum in depression.

Using PFM, they showed that the frontostriatal salience network occupied nearly twice as much cortical territory in depression, and importantly they determined that the effect was driven primarily by network boundary shifts/encroachment onto neighboring systems. ➤ PubMed +1

They explicitly quantified network surface-area contribution and examined which neighboring networks were displaced. ➤ PubMed Central (…)

So your proposed:

HC vs MDD individualized hippocampal system surface area + boundary/topography

has a very strong conceptual precedent.

It's essentially taking the Lynch question from:

“Is cortical network topology altered in depression?”

to:

“Is hippocampal functional-system topology altered in depression?”

3. Lynch also supports separating topology from connectivity

This is especially relevant to our last exchange.

Lynch distinguishes relatively stable network topology from changes in functional connectivity. Their salience-network expansion was stable across mood state, whereas frontostriatal connectivity changes tracked symptom fluctuations. ➤ PubMed Central (…)

That's very close to the distinction I was suggesting for you:

Functional topology

Where are the hippocampal systems? How large are they? How differentiated are they?

versus

Functional connectivity

Once those individualized systems are identified, how does each system communicate with cortical networks/ROIs?

So there is good precedent for treating those as related but distinct phenotypes rather than collapsing them into one analysis.

4. “Gradient compression” also has precedent in MDD—but not, as far as I can find, for your hippocampal question

There is already literature interpreting a narrower gradient range / reduced spatial variation as less differentiated connectome organization in MDD. For example, recent work on white-matter functional-connectome gradients reported a narrower gradient range and lower spatial variation in MDD and interpreted this as a less differentiated/contracted connectivity hierarchy. ➤ PubMed Central (…)

So if your individualized hippocampal gradient shows something like:

\[ HC:\quad \text{strong anterior} \rightarrow \text{posterior differentiation} \]

versus

\[ MDD:\quad \text{compressed gradient} \]

the conceptual interpretation of reduced functional differentiation has precedent in connectome-gradient research.

But I would be careful about claiming this precedent is hippocampal. I haven't found evidence yet that someone has done precisely individualized hippocampal PFM + gradient compression in MDD.

5. The part I now think is particularly promising is your template-margin analysis

There is broader PFM precedent for retaining probabilistic/support information rather than only hard labels. For example, modern PFM implementations can output per-network assignment/support probability maps specifically because winner-take-all labels can hide ambiguity between plausible network assignments. GitHub

That reinforces the logic behind what we're proposing.

Imagine two anterior hippocampal vertices:

\[ HC = [.72, .21, .08] \]
\[ MDD = [.48, .43, .12]. \]

Winner-take-all:

\[ HC = A,\qquad MDD = A \]

No apparent group difference.

But:

\[ \Delta HC = .51 \]
\[ \Delta MDD = .05. \]

Now you have a potentially very different biological result:

The anterior hippocampal territory remains spatially identifiable in MDD, but its functional identity is less differentiated from the neighboring hippocampal system.

I haven't found a previous hippocampal MDD paper doing exactly this.

And importantly, it isn't an arbitrary post-hoc metric. It's extracting information that already exists in the template-matching operation before winner-take-all discretization.

6. I would slightly modify one thing I suggested earlier

After looking more closely at the precedent, I would not immediately make entropy a major analysis.

I'd prioritize:

\[ \Delta r = r_{\mathrm{preferred}} - r_{\mathrm{next-best}} \]

because it's simple, directly tied to the template-matching algorithm, and biologically interpretable.

I'd also retain:

\[ r_{\mathrm{preferred}} \]

because these distinguish two different abnormalities.

For example:

Reduced \(r_{\mathrm{preferred}}\), preserved \(\Delta r\)
→ the vertex is less canonical overall but still clearly belongs to one system.

Preserved \(r_{\mathrm{preferred}}\), reduced \(\Delta r\)
→ competing system identity has increased.

Both reduced
→ weaker canonical identity plus poorer differentiation.

That could become substantially more informative than entropy alone.

7. Your cortical-network/ROI analysis also has a useful precedent

Kember already demonstrates that the three hippocampal systems possess distinct neocortical connectivity profiles and examines connectivity between the hippocampal systems and individually mapped cortical networks. ⓝ Nature

So the basic analysis:

\[ HC_{\mathrm{anterior/body/posterior}} \times \text{cortical network} \]

is well grounded.

Your extension would be:

\[ \mathbf{Group \times HC_{system} \times cortical\;network.} \]

Then ROI-level follow-up identifies which cortical nodes drive a network-level effect.

That's not methodologically exotic; what's interesting is performing it after individualized hippocampal template matching and individualized cortical PFM in HC versus MDD.

So I would now organize the independent HC/MDD analysis into three layers

I. Topography — Lynch precedent

System surface area
Boundary position/displacement
Spatial organization along long axis

↓

II. Functional identity — Kember method, but your extension

\(r_{\mathrm{preferred}}\)
\(r_{\mathrm{preferred}} - r_{\mathrm{second}}\)
possibly continuous gradient strength/range

↓

III. Circuit phenotype — Kember + MDD FC extension

HC system × cortical network connectivity
→ significant networks
→ cortical ROI localization

This gives you three separable questions:

Where is the system? — topology
How distinctly does that location express its expected functional identity? — differentiation
Which cortical circuitry is responsible for the altered identity? — connectivity fingerprint

And based on what I can find, #1 has strong precedent in depression PFM (Lynch), the methodological machinery for #2 exists in hippocampal PFM (Kember) but using the continuous competition among templates as an HC–MDD phenotype appears much less explored, and #3 has precedent for hippocampal-system connectivity but your individualized HC/MDD application would extend it. ⓝ Nature +1
