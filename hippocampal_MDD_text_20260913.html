Yes. I would take this one step beyond “which parcel does the vertex belong to?” and turn the template-similarity structure into a quantitative measure of hippocampal functional differentiation. That would let you ask whether MDD alters the organization of the hippocampus, rather than merely shifting parcel boundaries.

Kember's implementation already gives you the essential starting point: each hippocampal vertex has a connectivity profile to cortex, and each vertex is correlated with each normative hippocampal-system template. ⓝ Nature

1. Keep the three continuous similarity values

For every hippocampal vertex \(v\), don't retain only:

\[ \mathrm{label}(v) = \arg\max_k r_{vk} \]

Instead retain:

\[ r_{v,A},\quad r_{v,B},\quad r_{v,P} \]

for anterior, body, and posterior templates.

These three numbers contain much more information than the categorical assignment.

I would derive at least three vertex-level quantities.

A. Maximum template similarity

\[ S_{\max}(v) = \max(r_{v,A}, r_{v,B}, r_{v,P}) \]

This asks:

How strongly does this hippocampal location express any canonical hippocampal functional identity?

A reduction in MDD would indicate that hippocampal locations are less well represented by the normative functional organization.

B. Template separability / margin

For each vertex:

\[ M(v) = r_{\mathrm{highest}} - r_{\mathrm{second\;highest}} \]

This is probably the measure I would be most excited about.

A healthy vertex might be strongly anterior-like and poorly body-like:

\[ 0.65,\; 0.25,\; 0.10 \]

giving a large margin.

An MDD vertex might be:

\[ 0.39,\; 0.36,\; 0.27 \]

giving a small margin.

Both get the same categorical label, but the second vertex has much less distinct functional identity.

That is exactly the phenomenon you identified.

C. Assignment uncertainty

You can convert the three similarity values into normalized probabilities and calculate entropy:

\[ H(v) = -\sum_k p_{vk} \log p_{vk} \]

Higher entropy means that the vertex resembles multiple systems similarly; lower entropy means a more specific functional identity.

I would consider this a useful secondary analysis, but I would make margin or similarity contrast primary, because entropy introduces another transformation and is somewhat less immediately interpretable.

2. Move from vertices to the whole hippocampal organization

Once you have those maps, the next question is:

Is the hippocampus less functionally differentiated in MDD?

Calculate subject-level summaries within each hippocampus.

For example:

Overall differentiation

Mean \(M(v)\) across hippocampal vertices.

Prediction:

MDD < HC

This is a very clean test of reduced functional specialization.

System-specific differentiation

Within vertices assigned to each system, calculate:

\[ r_{\mathrm{own}} - r_{\mathrm{best\;alternative}} \]

For example, does the anterior system in MDD become less distinctly anterior-like?

This lets you distinguish:

global dedifferentiation

from

selective disruption of one hippocampal system.

3. Then examine the spatial organization itself

This is where your hard maps become useful again.

For every participant, calculate:

System surface area / proportion

\[ P_A,\; P_B,\; P_P \]

Does MDD have a different proportion of hippocampus assigned to the three systems?

This is analogous to Kember's developmental analysis of system surface area. ⓝ Nature

But I would not stop here, because area can change while the underlying functional organization remains very strong.

Boundary location

You can calculate the anterior–body and body–posterior boundary positions along your unfolded hippocampal surface.

Then ask whether the boundaries are systematically displaced in MDD.

This gives you:

topographic reorganization

rather than just:

reduced functional differentiation.

Those are different biological hypotheses.

4. I would add a particularly powerful analysis: longitudinal-axis organization

Because your atlas emerged from a gradient analysis, you have a natural continuous axis underlying the three clusters.

Rather than forcing the hippocampus into only three categories, calculate for each individual's hippocampus a continuous functional gradient score based on the same connectivity representation.

Then ask:

Is the HC gradient preserved in MDD?

For example:

\[ \mathrm{anterior\text{-}like} \rightarrow \mathrm{body\text{-}like} \rightarrow \mathrm{posterior\text{-}like} \]

In HC, you might see a smooth transition.

In MDD, several possibilities become testable:

1. Reduced gradient strength — the anterior/posterior functional distinction is weaker.

2. Boundary displacement — the transition occurs at a different anatomical location.

3. Gradient compression — large portions of hippocampus become functionally intermediate.

4. Noncanonical organization — some regions deviate from the expected longitudinal trajectory.

I think this is more mechanistically informative than simply reporting “MDD had a different hippocampal parcellation.”

5. Then ask whether the specific functional identities are altered

Your three templates aren't arbitrary parcels—they have distinct cortical-network fingerprints.

So for every subject/system, calculate its connectivity to the canonical cortical networks.

For example:

\[ HC_A \rightarrow \mathrm{salience/VAN} \]
\[ HC_B \rightarrow \mathrm{SMN/auditory/etc.} \]
\[ HC_P \rightarrow \mathrm{DMN} \]

Then ask:

Does an MDD individual's anterior hippocampal system still preferentially express the anterior template's cortical-network connectivity fingerprint?

This separates two possibilities:

Spatial disruption

The three systems retain their functional identities but occupy different locations.

versus

Functional identity disruption

The hippocampal system itself becomes less connected to the networks that normally define it.

That distinction is extremely valuable.

6. I would combine these into a “canonicality” framework

This may be the strongest overall analysis.

For each participant, quantify:

Canonicality
→ how well the individual's hippocampal vertices match the normative templates.

Differentiation
→ how strongly one template dominates over competing templates.

Topographic organization
→ whether the systems occupy the expected anterior–posterior locations.

Network specificity
→ whether each system retains its characteristic cortical-network connectivity.

You could end up with something conceptually like:

That tells a much richer story than “MDD differs in hippocampal connectivity.”
